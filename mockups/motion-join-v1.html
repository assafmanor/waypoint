Joining an invitation — the pass arrives, and gets stamped

A boarding pass is an object. Objects arrive, and they get stamped. Every beat here follows from that one sentence — which is why the strongest piece of visual writing in the app needs no celebration vocabulary of its own, only permission to behave like what it already looks like.

Play the flow

Two entry paths, one pass. Already signed in is the short version — the pass arrives and one tap stamps it. Anonymous is the long one, and it is the flow with the real problem.

Beat 2 — arrival is the whole first impression

Today the pass appears fully formed, which reads as a rendered page. Here it settles: the card comes up with a whisper of rotation, the notches punch (they are holes — a hole appears by being made), the countdown counts to its value, and the avatars land one after another on the shared stagger step.

The avatars are the sentence, not the decoration. "4 people are already in" is the social proof this screen runs on, and staggering them makes it read as people arriving rather than as a row that was always there. Same data, different claim.
The glow ramps with the pass. The shipped screen carries its amber anticipation glow from the first frame — warm before there is anything to be warm about. Ramping it on arrival makes the screen warm because of the invitation. Watch the top edge during beat 2.

Beat 3 — the stamp, and the tear

The counterpart to trip birth, and the beat that does not exist today — joinTrip() resolves and navigate('/') cuts. You are admitted: the pass is stamped, it recoils under the stamp, then it tears along the perforation and the stub hands off to the trip.

The tear costs nothing. .ticket-perf and its two .notch circles are already in the shipped CSS, sitting there as decoration promising an action. Tearing along it adds no element and redeems a detail the design already paid for.
Which half goes with you. The top half carries the trip's identity, so it is the half that rises into the handoff; the stub — the people, the part a gate agent keeps — is what leaves. Reversed, the motion says you were the one torn off.

Beat 3b — the anonymous detour is a design bug, not a motion gap

An unauthenticated visitor taps "Continue with Google", leaves for OAuth, and JoinTrip's effect auto-joins on return via consumeJoinIntent(). So the app makes the decision while the user is looking at a redirect and they arrive inside a trip having never seen a confirmation.

Visible resumecome back to the pass, then stamp it
Turn it off and play Anonymous: the return lands already-joined, which is what ships. On, the return re-shows the pass you already saw and then stamps it — the same one explicit tap ADR-0024 specifies, now visible. It costs one beat and it is the difference between being admitted and being processed.

Beat 4 — refused

An expired or revoked invite (ADR-0067) is today a paragraph — and a paragraph on a loading screen reads as a loading state that never resolved, not as a decision. A rejection should look like one: the pass is drawn, struck through, and stamped פג תוקף, the anticipation glow drops out, and the next action is offered.

Draw the trip, then strike it. Rendering the pass you cannot use is deliberate: it tells you the invitation was real and the link is what expired — so the next step is "ask for a new link", not "check the address". Withholding it leaves the user unable to tell a dead link from a typo.
Not the same as removed. ADR-0067's TripBlock case (an admin kicked you) must not reuse this — the same stamp for "the link aged out" and "you were removed" would be the app declining to say which. It needs its own copy, which is a copy decision this file does not make.

One thing the stamp must not become. It is applied on the server's success, never optimistically — a stamp that has to be un-stamped when joinTrip fails is worse than no stamp. The spinner covers the request; the stamp only lands on a real membership.
